Pentagon Terminates Anthropic’s $200M Contract, Pivots to Google, OpenAI, and SpaceX for Battlefield AI

The Department of Defense has formally labeled Anthropic a “supply chain risk” and terminated its $200 million defense contract after CEO Dario Amodei refused to remove safety guardrails governing Claude’s deployment in classified Pentagon systems. The dispute centers on Anthropic’s opposition to two specific use cases: fully autonomous armed drones and AI-assisted mass surveillance capable of tracking domestic dissent. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and the Trump administration accused the company of jeopardizing national security; Anthropic has filed a lawsuit alleging the Pentagon is unlawfully retaliating by applying a designation designed to protect against foreign adversaries. U.S. News & World Report and Action News Jax confirmed that the Pentagon has barred other government contractors from working with Anthropic, effectively cutting the firm out of the defense industrial base.

The designation carries significant operational consequences beyond this single contract. By applying the supply chain risk label — a designation previously reserved for foreign-origin hardware threats — the DoD created a precedent that any AI vendor with ethical-use restrictions on its model cards could face exclusion from federal prime or subcontract work. The Pentagon has explicitly repositioned its AI strategy around Anthropic’s competitors: Google, OpenAI, and SpaceX are now the preferred providers for AI that can “enhance warfighter decision-making in complex operational environments.” The move occurs against a backdrop of broader debate; several senior military leaders publicly urged caution on autonomous targeting, even as Hegseth pushed for speed.

For federal procurement professionals and BD teams, the episode illustrates that AI vendor ethics policies are now a material contracting variable. Agencies evaluating AI tools — even for non-defense applications — must now weigh whether a model provider’s acceptable-use restrictions are compatible with the agency’s mission requirements. Simultaneously, the American AI Accountability Act (passed Senate Commerce 14-8 on May 28) would impose third-party safety audits and $50M/violation penalties in healthcare, law enforcement, and critical infrastructure — creating a future regulatory environment that would mandate the very controls the Pentagon is currently penalizing. This tension between DoD’s permissive posture and Congress’s emerging oversight regime will be the central fault line of federal AI acquisition for the next 18 months.

Any proposals involving Claude or Anthropic tooling should now include a vendor risk mitigation section addressing DoD supply chain designation risks, even for civilian agency work where cross-agency perception matters.

Anthropic Overtakes OpenAI in Valuation After $65B Series H at $900B+

Anthropic has closed a $65 billion Series H funding round valuing the company above $900 billion, surpassing OpenAI’s $730 billion valuation to become the most valuable private AI company on Earth. The round — closed in the same week the company’s Pentagon contract was terminated — underscores that investor confidence in Anthropic remains high despite the government contract loss. Separately, Anthropic dropped Claude Opus 4.8 just 42 days after 4.7, the shortest gap between Opus releases ever, signaling an accelerating model cadence. Both OpenAI and Anthropic are now widely expected to pursue IPOs later in 2026.

Google Gemini Spark Goes Live — 24/7 Agentic Assistant Runs in the Background

Google activated Gemini Spark on May 29 for US-based Google AI Ultra subscribers, one week after its debut at I/O 2026. Spark is not a chatbot tab but a persistent agent built on Gemini 3.5 and Google’s Antigravity harness; it runs continuously in the cloud, monitors Gmail and Calendar, drafts documents, and can browse websites through a remote browser — even when the user’s device is off. Deep out-of-the-box integration with the full Google Workspace suite (Gmail, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Drive) gives Spark an immediate advantage over rivals that require manual setup. For federal agencies already on Google Workspace, Spark represents the first enterprise-grade autonomous agent available without custom integration work.

OpenAI Publishes Frontier Governance Framework — Pre-Positioning Before Regulation

OpenAI released its Frontier Governance Framework on May 29, a public document mapping its safety practices to incoming regulation across four risk domains: cyber offense, CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear) risk, harmful manipulation, and loss-of-control scenarios. The release is widely read as the company proactively setting the terms for how its practices will be evaluated under the American AI Accountability Act and similar legislation. For federal procurement officers, the framework provides the first publicly available OpenAI safety self-assessment that can be referenced in source-selection criteria and risk management plans — a practical tool as agencies update AI acquisition playbooks.

Source: Agentic AI Hype (Substack)

Cognition AI Banks $1B; GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.8 Reach Competitive Parity on Coding Tasks

Cognition AI (maker of the Devin software engineering agent) has closed a $1 billion funding round, placing it among the best-capitalized AI coding companies. The raise coincides with a broad consensus in the developer community that GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 have effectively reached parity on most coding benchmarks, with differentiation increasingly driven by tooling ecosystem, pricing, and agentic harness quality rather than raw model capability. The practical implication for federal IT contractors: vendor lock-in decisions made today around a specific model will be less about raw capability and more about the surrounding workflow infrastructure (Claude Code, Codex, Azure AI Foundry) and long-term support posture.
