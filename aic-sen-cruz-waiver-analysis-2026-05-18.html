AIC / Sen. Cruz Waiver Language Analysis

Mary Pritchard’s Four Proposed Edits — Changeis Strategic Assessment
Created: 2026-05-18  ·  Prepared for: Varun Malhotra / Changeis  ·  Context: AIC Board Review of Sen. Cruz BNATCS Permitting Waiver Draft

Executive Summary

Support Mary’s Package with One Refinement

Mary Pritchard’s four proposed edits collectively strengthen the BNATCS permitting waiver language in ways that are legally sound, strategically defensible, and unlikely to create coalition friction at the AIC board level. Three of the four edits (Items #1, #2, and #3) are clear improvements that Changeis should endorse without qualification. The fourth (Item #4) raises a legitimate structural question that benefits from modest clarification rather than a binary accept/reject decision.

The underlying strategic tension in the draft is a classic one in infrastructure legislation: the Senate side will push to minimize procedural encumbrances on the Administrator, while the AIC board (and particularly Changeis) has a valid interest in ensuring that transparency mechanisms survive implementation intact. Mary’s edits correctly identify where the current draft erodes those mechanisms. Varun’s strongest position is to frame support for Mary’s package as a pro-implementation posture — good guardrails make the waiver politically durable, not just legally defensible.

Change-by-Change Analysis

1
Item #4 — Strike “Reporting should be informational only and should not delay waiver effectiveness” Consolidated List, Item #4
Changeis Position: Support

Mary’s Rationale (As Stated)

The reporting mechanism AIC added IS the transparency guardrail. Characterizing it as “informational only” undermines its weight. Moreover, the Administrator initiates reporting (he controls it), unlike review processes where he waits on other actors — so the “delay” concern is misplaced.

Substantive Merit

Mary is correct on both counts. The phrase “informational only” in statutory text is a term of art that courts interpret to mean the output has no operative legal consequence — it cannot trigger requirements, affect rights, or form the basis for challenge. If reporting is the primary transparency and accountability mechanism for a permitting waiver that bypasses notice-and-comment, characterizing it as “informational only” hollows it out. The second clause (“should not delay waiver effectiveness”) compounds the problem by signaling legislative intent that no procedural obligation can slow issuance — including the reporting itself. Since the Administrator initiates and controls reporting timing, the delay concern has no factual basis; the clause is drafting surplusage at best, corrosive surplusage at worst.

Likely Senate Reaction

Moderate resistance, not outright opposition. The Senate Commerce Committee staff likely inserted this language to reassure BNATCS operators that the permitting waiver would not get caught in procedural limbo. Their counterargument will be that “informational only” prevents the reporting requirement from being weaponized in litigation to challenge waiver effectiveness. This is a genuine concern. AIC should acknowledge it and offer the alternative fix (see Item #4 analysis for the cross-reference solution) rather than simply striking without replacement.

Risk if Accepted
Low. Improves legal coherence. Reporting has defined teeth. No operational disruption.
Risk if Rejected (language stays)
High. A future Administrator or DOT General Counsel could invoke “informational only” to argue that the reporting obligation is merely advisory, effectively rendering the guardrail optional. Litigation exposure if a waiver is challenged and the government points to this language to defend non-compliance with reporting timelines.
Additional Drafting Note

If Senate staff pushes back, AIC can offer a middle-ground substitute: “Reporting shall be provided to Congress pursuant to Sec. 40133(c) and shall not constitute a condition precedent to waiver effectiveness.” This preserves the reporting obligation’s legal standing while addressing the delay concern operationally rather than substantively.

2
Page 1, Line #5 — Strike “and for other purposes” Page 1, Line 5
Changeis Position: Support

Mary’s Rationale (As Stated)

The phrase opens the door to FAA using the permitting waiver authority for purposes beyond BNATCS, undermining the BNATCS-specific guardrails the AIC board negotiated.

Substantive Merit

Mary is right and this is a significant structural vulnerability. “And for other purposes” is a catch-all title phrase commonly added in omnibus legislation to enable bill managers to bundle unrelated provisions. In a freestanding or targeted piece like a BNATCS permitting waiver, the phrase serves no legitimate drafting function but creates genuine risk: it signals that the grant of authority extends beyond the stated subject matter. Courts applying expressio unius would normally confine the waiver to BNATCS; but the catch-all undermines that inference. More practically, future FAA Administrators could cite “for other purposes” as authority to apply waiver provisions to non-BNATCS infrastructure programs, eroding the specificity of the AIC-negotiated framework.

Likely Senate Reaction

Minimal resistance. This phrase was almost certainly added by bill managers as a placeholder and has no substantive champion. Senate Commerce Committee staff are unlikely to fight its removal; it is a cleanup edit that costs them nothing. If resistance does emerge, it may come from within FAA’s Office of General Counsel, which may prefer the flexibility — but that is an executive branch concern, not a legislative one.

Risk if Accepted
Near-zero. Tightens scope of authority to what AIC negotiated. No downside.
Risk if Rejected (language stays)
Medium. Provides a hook for scope creep. If a future program (e.g., advanced air mobility infrastructure, drone corridors) invokes this waiver authority, AIC will have inadvertently helped create a broader deregulatory instrument than intended.
Drafting Note

When presenting this to the board, frame it affirmatively: removing “and for other purposes” does not constrain FAA’s existing statutory authorities — it simply ensures this new waiver authority is confined to the BNATCS context where AIC’s guardrails apply. If FAA needs broader authority for other infrastructure, that requires a separate legislative grant with its own safeguards.

3
Page 3, Line #16 — Grammar Fix: “an” + plural mismatch Page 3, Line 16
Changeis Position: Support (with preference for “any changes”)

Mary’s Rationale (As Stated)

The text reads “an [change/changes]” — a grammatical mismatch between article and noun. Either “an change” (singular, grammatically incorrect) or “any changes” (plural, grammatically correct) is needed.

Substantive Merit

This is a necessary correction but the choice of fix has interpretive consequences. “Any changes” (plural) is broader and captures a series of modifications. “A change” (singular) would restrict the provision to individual, discrete alterations and could be interpreted to require separate compliance actions for each change. In the BNATCS context, where the Administrator will be making multiple infrastructure modifications under a waiver, “any changes” is the operationally correct fix — it avoids a reading where each individual change requires a separate notice or report. Changeis should advocate explicitly for “any changes” rather than leaving the choice open.

Likely Senate Reaction

No resistance expected. This is a cleanup correction and no senator will go to the mat over an article-noun agreement. However, staff should be briefed that the fix is “any changes” specifically (not “a change”), and the reason should be in the record to foreclose a narrow reading later.

Risk if Accepted (“any changes”)
Low. Broadest, operationally correct reading. Consistent with scope of BNATCS implementation.
Risk if Fixed as “a change”
Medium. Could be read to require individual reporting events for each discrete BNATCS modification — creating an unintended administrative burden or, conversely, providing a hook to challenge bulk modifications as not individually reported.
Recommended Fix

Substitute “any changes” (plural with “any” as the article/quantifier). Add a note to the record: “The correction to ‘any changes’ is consistent with the Administrator’s authority under the waiver to implement multiple coordinated BNATCS modifications and is not intended to require separate reporting for each discrete operational change.”

4
Page 5, Line #28 — Reporting reference in notice-and-comment carve-out Page 5, Line 28
Changeis Position: Support with Modification

Mary’s Rationale (As Stated)

The current notice-and-comment carve-out provision on Page 5 does not explicitly cross-reference the reporting requirement in Sec. 40133(c). Mary proposes making explicit: “shall conform to the reporting requirement specified in Sec. 40133(c)… but shall not be subject to notice-and-comment rulemaking requirements.”

Substantive Merit

This is the most sophisticated of Mary’s four edits and she is strategically correct. The structural problem in the current draft is that the notice-and-comment carve-out (exempting BNATCS permitting actions from APA 5 U.S.C. §553 procedures) stands as a blanket negative — “not subject to notice-and-comment” — without a corresponding positive obligation that fills the transparency gap. By explicitly cross-referencing Sec. 40133(c) reporting requirements within the same provision, the draft accomplishes two things: (1) it signals legislative intent that reporting is the substitute transparency mechanism, not an add-on; (2) it creates a legal nexus that makes it much harder for a future Administrator to read the notice-and-comment exemption as also exempting the reporting obligation. This is sound legislative architecture. The only question is whether the cross-reference language is sufficient as written or needs a conjunctive structure to make the obligation clearer.

Likely Senate Reaction

The Senate will likely accept the structural concept (reporting as the APA substitute) but may resist specific cross-reference language if it appears to create a new enforceable standard within the carve-out provision. Senate staff may prefer a recital or a finding rather than operative cross-reference language. AIC should be prepared to negotiate the form (cross-reference vs. findings clause) while holding firm on the substance (reporting is explicitly the transparency substitute for notice-and-comment).

Risk if Accepted as Proposed
Low-medium. If the cross-reference is insufficiently precise, it could create an ambiguity about which subsection of Sec. 40133(c) governs. Specify subsection, content requirements, and timing.
Risk if Rejected (no cross-reference)
High. A blanket notice-and-comment carve-out with no explicit positive obligation leaves a structural gap that erodes the entire guardrail framework. Courts interpreting the exemption broadly could read reporting as also optional. This is the single highest-stakes drafting issue in the package.
Recommended Language

Support Mary’s concept but strengthen the cross-reference. Suggested formulation:

“Any permitting action authorized under this section shall conform to the reporting requirements specified in Sec. 40133(c), including the content, timing, and transmission requirements set forth therein, but shall not be subject to the notice-and-comment rulemaking requirements of 5 U.S.C. §553. Compliance with the reporting requirements of Sec. 40133(c) shall constitute the transparency mechanism required for any such action in lieu of notice-and-comment procedures.”

This language (a) makes reporting explicitly mandatory and tied to a specific statutory provision, (b) clarifies that the notice-and-comment exemption does not reach reporting, and (c) creates a legislative record that reporting is the substitute for — not merely a supplement to — notice-and-comment for these permitting actions.

APA Architecture Note

Under 5 U.S.C. §553(b)(A) and (B), interpretive rules and rules with good cause are already exempt from notice-and-comment. If the BNATCS permitting actions at issue are characterized as interpretive or procedural, the carve-out is legally redundant but politically important for clarity. If they are characterized as substantive rules, the carve-out is legally necessary — making the cross-reference to reporting as the substitute mechanism even more critical for the provision’s defensibility against APA challenges.

Additional Edits Mary May Have Missed

Based on the commentary and the structural logic of the four proposed changes, the following gaps in the underlying language warrant AIC board consideration:

Gap A — No Sunset or Review Trigger on Waiver Authority

Mary’s edits collectively strengthen what the reporting mechanism does, but none address whether the waiver authority itself has a defined endpoint or review trigger. If the waiver authority is permanent (no sunset), Congress loses the periodic reauthorization leverage that would otherwise allow adjustment of guardrails as BNATCS evolves. Consider adding a 3-year review provision: the Administrator must report on waiver utilization and the congressional committees must either reauthorize or allow the authority to lapse. This is consistent with how the OBBB $12.5B was structured with phase-gate accountability.

Gap B — Scope of “BNATCS Permitting Actions” is Undefined

The draft appears to grant a blanket permitting waiver for BNATCS without defining which categories of FAA permitting actions are covered. “BNATCS permitting” could be read to cover only construction/site permits or could extend to operational approvals, equipment certifications, and contractor compliance certifications. Without a defined scope, the waiver authority is as broad as the Administrator’s interpretation. AIC should push for an enumerated list or a defined term (“Covered BNATCS Permitting Actions means [list]”) that bounds the authority.

Gap C — No Minimum Reporting Content Requirements in Statute

Mary’s edits assume the reporting requirement in Sec. 40133(c) has sufficient content specificity. If that provision only says “the Administrator shall report to Congress,” without specifying what the report must contain (e.g., which waivers were granted, on what basis, to which entities, with what safety analysis), the reporting obligation is structurally weak regardless of how well it is cross-referenced. AIC should confirm that Sec. 40133(c) has adequate content requirements — and if not, advocate for a minimum content specification (waiver number, scope, basis, affected facilities, timeline, any safety deviations).

Gap D — No Delegation Constraint on Waiver Authority

The current draft appears to vest waiver authority in “the Administrator” without restricting sub-delegation. If the Administrator can delegate waiver authority to regional FAA offices or to the Peraton prime integrator team, the accountability structure collapses. Consider a provision: “The Administrator may not further delegate the authority granted under this section to any person outside the Federal Aviation Administration.” This is standard practice in statutes granting significant deregulatory authority.

All four edits flow from the same principle: the APA notice-and-comment exemption granted for BNATCS permitting speed must be matched by equally specific positive obligations (reporting, content, timing) if the waiver is to survive both congressional scrutiny and potential legal challenge. Charles Keegan’s approval of the package as-is is reasonable; Changeis’ contribution is to add precision on Item #4 and flag the four structural gaps above.

Strategic Positioning for Varun’s Board Response

Core Framing

Varun should frame Changeis’ position not as “agreeing with Mary” but as taking the independent position that implementation durability requires structural guardrails. The argument is not about slowing BNATCS — it is about making the waiver authority legally and politically durable. A permitting waiver that gets challenged in court or reversed by a future administration because its transparency mechanisms were hollow is worse for BNATCS than the one-time friction of getting the language right now.

What to Emphasize

  • The AIC board negotiated these guardrails in the first round; Mary’s edits preserve them. Walking them back now is not a concession to speed — it is a concession of accountability.
  • The Senate side’s legitimate concern (that reporting not delay waivers) can be addressed through form, not by eliminating the substance of the obligation.
  • The four structural gaps (sunset, scope definition, content requirements, delegation constraint) are the next-level work — framing Changeis as forward-leaning on implementation rigor, not reactive.
  • Charles Keegan’s approval is useful political cover: the edit package now has backing from two board members before Varun weighs in, giving Varun’s endorsement the character of a consensus seal rather than a contentious position.

What to Avoid

  • Avoid framing this as “the Senate wants to gut guardrails.” The more accurate framing is “the current draft language is ambiguous in ways that will be exploited administratively, and we are proposing precision fixes.”
  • Avoid getting pulled into a debate about whether BNATCS needs permitting streamlining — the AIC board is aligned on that. The debate is only about the form of the streamlining mechanism.

Suggested Talking Points for Varun’s Reply

  • On the package overall: “Mary has identified four edits that strengthen rather than complicate the permitting waiver language. All four are consistent with AIC’s stated objective of BNATCS-specific guardrails — they ensure the guardrails we negotiated actually function as intended when implemented.”
  • On Item #1 (informational only): “Characterizing the reporting obligation as ‘informational only’ would give any future Administrator’s general counsel a textual basis for treating it as advisory. That is the opposite of the accountability structure we built. If the Senate is concerned about delay, the fix is a non-condition-precedent clause — not degrading the reporting obligation’s legal standing.”
  • On Item #2 (for other purposes): “This is a cleanup. The phrase ‘for other purposes’ has no substantive advocate and creates a scope problem for our BNATCS-specific framework. Its removal costs nothing and removes a hook for authority creep.”
  • On Item #4 (cross-reference): “Mary is right on the concept: the notice-and-comment carve-out needs an explicit positive counterweight. I’d suggest we strengthen the cross-reference language to specify content, timing, and the substitution rationale — and flag this as the highest-stakes drafting issue in the package for Senate negotiations.”
  • On the structural gaps: “Beyond Mary’s four edits, I want to flag four additional gaps the board should consider: no sunset on waiver authority, undefined scope of covered actions, absent minimum reporting content requirements, and no delegation constraint. These are second-round issues but worth naming now so we can address them before the bill hardens.”
